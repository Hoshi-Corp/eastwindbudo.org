Jujutsu nearly didn't survive the twentieth century. By the late 1800s, only a handful of schools remained in Japan — Jigoro Kano's new art of Judo had absorbed much of jujutsu's audience, karate's arrival gave students another option, and World War II cost the tradition several senior teachers and destroyed a number of its makimono, the hand scrolls schools used to transmit technique.

What we teach — Yamanaka-ha Shindō-ryū — descends from Yoshin-ryū, taught in the mid-to-late 1800s by Hirotsuke Totsuka. His student Katsunosuke Matsuoka founded Shindō Yōshin-ryū, incorporating technique from multiple older styles; that line passed through Yokiyoshi Tatsusaburō Nakayama before reaching the teachers below.

It's a grappling art at heart — using an opponent's own force and motion against them, to pin, lock, throw, and control rather than strike.
